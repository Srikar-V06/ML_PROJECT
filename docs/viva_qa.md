# Viva Q&A — Blocks 1, 2 & 3

> *Note on cohort evolution:* Questions 4–6 in Block 1 reflect P1's initial 200k-loan diagnostic run (`commit 4bdd5cf`); in the final P1/P2 handoff (`commit 2f9572e`), `lc_loan.csv.zip` borrower linkage was added to enforce **300,000 distinct verified borrowers** (`210k` Train / `45k` Val / `45k` Test) and a 50-field origination budget (`splits/p1p2_300k/`). Both sets of numbers are preserved below so nothing from Block 1 or Block 2 is lost.

## Block 1

1. **Why exclude Current?** Its terminal outcome is unknown. Calling it good would reward vintages with insufficient follow-up.
2. **Why 2016Q1?** It is the last quarter of the contiguous window passing the 90% 36-month resolution rule. The cutoff quarter achieved 93.41%.
3. **What remains censored?** Only 58.65% of 60-month loans at the cutoff were resolved. Early exits can have different risks from loans still active.
4. **What does 200k (and the final 300k handoff) represent?** The initial 200k cohort was a proportional sample of 938,540 eligible accepted and resolved loans; the final 300k cohort draws 300,000 distinct borrowers (`member_id` verified unique via `audit.parquet`, excluding prior experiment IDs in `excluded_loan_ids.parquet`). Rejected applicants have no observed outcome here.
5. **How close was the sample?** Maximum PSI was 0.0000643 and maximum annual rate gap 0.001029 in the initial draw (and verified in `artifacts/p1p2_300k/sampling_representativeness.csv` for the 300k draw). PSI uses binned distributions; continuous KS uses the empirical distributions.
6. **Can the same person appear twice?** In the raw public CSV `member_id` was blank, so loan IDs alone could not rule out repeat borrowers; in the 300k handoff, `src/data/splits.py:verify_borrowers` links `member_id` from `lc_loan.csv.zip` (`audit.parquet`) and asserts 1-to-1 borrower uniqueness across all 300,000 rows so zero borrowers straddle Train, Val, and Test.
7. **How do you catch leakage that looks predictive?** Inventory timing first, screen one feature at a time on Train and then deliberately contaminate a diagnostic fit. Its validation AUC was 0.99964.
8. **Why keep almost-empty variables?** Coverage can encode timing, product or borrower context. 29 were empty throughout Train in the 99-field inventory; in the 50-field handoff, 21 core underwriting fields plus the 29 highest-coverage varying numeric bureau fields are retained with fold-local missing indicators.
9. **Does missingness prove MNAR?** The two-proportion test measures an association in observed defaults. It cannot establish the missing-data mechanism or rule out vintage confounding.
10. **Why both OOT and expanding CV?** OOT tests transfer to later vintages. The four Train-only folds permit repeated model development without accessing final assessment rows.
11. **How does one scorecard point arise?** Each bin contributes −(20/ln 2) × coefficient × WoE. Add the intercept-adjusted base. Doubling good/bad odds adds 20 points.
12. **How did you prevent target leakage in WoE?** Fit boundaries, WoE, IV selection and logistic coefficients on Train. A value unseen in later data uses its learned bin or a neutral fallback.
13. **Why paired DeLong?** The same loans receive both scores. Paired placements retain covariance. Scorecard versus full logistic produced p=1.95e-07 (and p=1.66e-21 on the 45k 300k Test cohort); paired bootstrap also compares AP and Brier.
14. **What does a 10:1 cost ratio buy?** It values one false negative at ten false positives. The locked threshold balances missed-default losses against foregone interest on rejected good loans. Real exposure and foregone margin determine actual dollars.
15. **Can P2 apply the scorecard directly to Prosper?** Only 17 underwriting fields have mapped counterparts. A transfer experiment requires harmonized categories and units, a common-feature model and assessment of label and vintage shift.

## Block 2

1. **Why multiply installment by twelve?** Installment is a monthly payment and income is annual. The ratio expresses the share of reported income committed to this loan. A zero income leaves that ratio undefined.
2. **What does a tree split optimize?** The default Gini criterion favors a split whose children have purer outcome distributions. Entropy is another measure of class uncertainty. Minimum leaf size limits how small a segment can become, and cost-complexity pruning penalizes extra leaves.
3. **Why use out-of-bag estimates and expanding folds?** Each bootstrap tree omits some training loans, so those trees can score their omitted loans. This measures internal forest performance. Expanding folds answer a different question about performance on subsequently issued loans.
4. **Why can two importance rankings disagree?** Impurity importance rewards features that yield many useful training splits. Permutation importance measures validation AUC lost when one input is shuffled. A correlated substitute can keep the AUC high.
5. **Why might 95% explained variance still lose predictive information?** PCA learns orthogonal directions that capture variation in inputs. A low-variance direction can carry default signal, and rotation can turn a single underwriting cutoff into several tree splits. Compare each PCA model against its matched control on Val (and on Test, `pca_lightgbm_80` dropped to 0.7100 ROC vs 0.7425 raw LightGBM, whereas `pca_lr_95` stayed at 0.7349 vs 0.7372 raw Logistic).

## Block 3

1. **Why did you use chronological 90/10 Train-tail early stopping (`round(best_iter / 0.9)`) instead of early-stopping on Validation or using `HistGradientBoostingClassifier(early_stopping=True)`?**
   Passing `Val` as an early-stopping `eval_set` leaks validation labels into every model's tree count and makes `Val` optimistically biased when we subsequently use `Val` to rank models, choose ensemble members, fit post-hoc calibrators (P4), and lock cost thresholds. Meanwhile, sklearn's `HistGradientBoostingClassifier(early_stopping=True)` carves out a *random* internal validation split from `Train`, leaking future macro regimes into earlier loans. Instead, `fit_with_train_tail_early_stopping` fits on the earliest 90% of `Train` (`189,000` loans) and evaluates on the most recent 10% chronological tail (`21,000` loans, using `staged_predict_proba` for HGB/AdaBoost/classic GB). Refitting on 100% of `Train` scales rounds by `round(best_iter / 0.9)` because each gradient step sees `1/0.9 ≈ 1.11×` more training data with slightly lower variance, matching effective regularization capacity while keeping `Val` 100% out-of-sample.

2. **Why did you treat P2's Optuna JSONs as Trial 0 warm starts rather than final hyperparameters?**
   P2's overnight check-in (`artifacts/p1p2_300k/block2/search_*.json`) ran 3 trials on `≤3,000` rows per fold (`1.4%` of Train) without early stopping, yielding inner-fold AUCs of `0.680–0.698` — well below the untuned `logreg_baseline` (`0.7473` Val ROC). Enqueuing P2's `params` via `study.enqueue_trial` preserved P2's work as Trial 0 while allowing P3's widened TPE search (`25,000` rows/fold, up to 30 trials / 25 min with fold-local early stopping) to lift inner-fold CV AUC to `0.712–0.713` and Test ROC-AUC to `0.742–0.744`.

3. **What did the 3-arm LightGBM missing-value study show, and why does median imputation barely affect GBDTs?**
   Holding tuned hyperparameters fixed (`num_leaves=9, lr=0.0137`), `lgbm_native_flags` (`Test ROC 0.7425`), `lgbm_native` (`0.7424`), and `lgbm_imputed_flags` (`0.7420`) differed by only `0.0005` ROC-AUC. Histogram GBDTs natively route `NaN` values to whichever child node (`left` or `right`) maximizes gain at every split, automatically learning missingness-as-signal interactions without needing explicit binary flags. Median imputation slightly hurts (`-0.0004` to `-0.0005` ROC) because collapsing missing bureau histories onto the median pools non-random missing borrowers with average-risk borrowers unless an extra split first isolates the binary indicator.

4. **Why did CatBoost outperform one-hot GBDTs on ROC-AUC (`0.7442` vs `0.7429` XGBoost, DeLong $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0033$)?**
   `CatBoostNativePreprocessor` passes the 5 categorical columns (`grade, sub_grade, home_ownership, verification_status, purpose`) natively as strings (`cat_features`). CatBoost computes online, permutation-ordered target statistics (`prior`-smoothed default rates on preceding rows) and builds symmetric **oblivious trees** (`depth=7`, same split across an entire level = `128` leaves/tree), which act as regularized global feature-interaction tables over ordered credit grades (`35` sub-grades) without fragmenting leaf sample sizes across sparse one-hot columns.

5. **What did `class_weights`, `undersample`, and `SMOTE` do to ranking versus probability calibration, and why does that break cost-sensitive lending?**
   On both `xgboost` and `catboost`, `class_weights` changed Test PR-AUC by at most `+0.0003` (`0.4276 → 0.4279`) and `undersample` *reduced* PR-AUC by `-0.002` (`discarding 135k majority loans`), while **both wrecked probability calibration**: mean predicted probability jumped from `~0.18` to `~0.45` against a `0.199` Test default rate, `Test Brier` jumped from `0.140` to `0.199–0.203`, and `Test ECE` surged `8–10×` (`0.024 → 0.239–0.244`). Under a 10:1 FN:FP cost ratio, theoretical threshold $t^* = 1/(1+10) = 0.091$ assumes calibrated probabilities; applying $0.091$ to `class_weights` scores would reject nearly every borrower! Meanwhile, plain `SMOTE` inside `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline` (applied strictly inside the training fold after imputation + one-hot encoding, preventing test leakage) lost `-0.0017` to `-0.0036` Test PR-AUC without inflating real-borrower $\hat{p}$ (`~0.190`), because Euclidean interpolation between discrete `{0, 1}` one-hot vectors places synthetic defaults at fractional coordinates (`0.3, 0.7`) that tree splits isolate from real `{0, 1}` test borrowers.

6. **How did you validate and verify `lgbm_monotone`, why `fico_range_low` instead of `fico_mean`, and why can raw `annual_inc` still behave non-monotonically?**
   First, `feature_audit.csv` retains only `fico_range_low` (`fico_range_high` is redundant, $r > 0.999$, and `fico_mean` does not exist in the 50-field schema). Second, we checked univariate default rates across `Train` deciles before fitting: all 7 features showed near-perfect monotonicity (`|Spearman ρ| ≥ 0.988`). Third, `lgbm_monotone` paid only **`-0.0010` Test ROC-AUC** (`0.7414` vs `0.7424` `lgbm_native`) and **`-0.0009` Test PR-AUC** ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.51$). Fourth, our 2,000-row ICE sweep verified `0/2,000` violations on 6 constrained model inputs (`70/2,000` tiny `≤5.8e-4` numerical steps on `annual_inc` under `monotone_constraints_method="advanced"`), *but* varying **raw** `annual_inc` violated monotonicity on `1,770/2,000` rows because changing raw income simultaneously changes `loan_to_income` (`+1`), `payment_to_income` (`+1`), and the **unconstrained** `revolving_to_income` (`0`). In regulated credit modeling, you must constrain every coupled ratio of a protected/governed input.

7. **Why couldn't you use `sklearn.ensemble.StackingClassifier` directly, and why did `stack_lr` cut Test ECE to `0.0045`?**
   `sklearn.ensemble.StackingClassifier` calls `cross_val_predict`, which requires the CV splitter's validation folds to form an exact partition of `X`. Forward-chaining time-series splits (`TimeSeriesSplit` / `train_expanding_cv.parquet`) cannot predict the earliest `42,000` loans (`2007–2011`) without past training data, and using a standard K-fold would train Fold-2..5 base models on *future* 2014–2015 loans to score 2008–2010 loans (temporal leakage). Our forward-chained stacker refit `xgb_hist`, `catboost`, and `logreg_baseline` strictly on each expanding fold's past partition to generate `168,000` out-of-time OOF logits (`rows 42k–210k`). Fitting `LogisticRegression(C=1.0)` on those OOF logits learned positive weights on all three diverse families (`0.651` CatBoost + `0.255` LogReg + `0.181` XGBoost) **plus an intercept (`+0.2685`) fitted on forward-time OOF shifts**, naturally correcting the upward drift in default rates from early Train (`17.7%`) to 2015 Test (`19.9%`) and cutting Test ECE from `0.0242` (`catboost`) to `0.0045` (`Brier = 0.1386`).

8. **How did you prevent Test-set snooping and multiple-testing false positives?**
   Before touching Test, `run_block3_f1_freeze.py` locked all 26 candidate models, their SHA-256 file hashes, the 50-column feature order, Val-selected cost thresholds, and the Val-chosen Champion (`stack_lr`) in `artifacts/p3/model_manifest.json`. `src/final_benchmark.py` verified every hash, wrote `artifacts/p3/test_lock.json` (which raises `RuntimeError` if invoked again), and scored the 45,000 Test borrowers once with 1,000 paired bootstrap resamples and paired DeLong tests. Across both the 25 Champion-vs-all comparisons and the 7 pre-registered pairs, we applied the **Holm-Bonferroni step-down procedure** (`p_holm`), controlling family-wise error rate uniformly with strictly higher power than standard Bonferroni.

9. **Why did you measure both `tracemalloc` and process RSS in Analysis C, and why were slow models timed at `n=1`?**
   Python's `tracemalloc` hooks only the Python allocator (`PyMem_Malloc`); C++ native buffers allocated by LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost (`histograms`, `QuantizedPool`) bypass Python completely (`infer_tracemalloc ≈ 2.8 MB` vs actual subprocess `infer_rss ≈ 219–339 MB`, converted from bytes on macOS `ru_maxrss`). Running each model in a fresh isolated subprocess captured true high-water RSS alongside `tracemalloc`. Models refitting in `<120s` used the median of `3` refits (`4` threads); models taking `≥120s` (`catboost` `126.7s`, `adaboost` `160.9s`, `stack_lr` `635.3s` OOF+meta) were run once (`n=1`) and explicitly flagged in `efficiency.csv` rather than hidden.

10. **Which model lies on the Pareto frontier and which would you deploy in production?**
    On the **inference-latency Pareto frontier** (`1` thread), `logreg_baseline` (`5.7 ms/1k`, `PR 0.4157`), `xgb_hist` (`12.6 ms/1k`, `13.4s` fit, `0.40 MB`, `PR 0.4264`), and `catboost` (`19.4 ms/1k`, `126.7s` fit, `1.55 MB`, `PR 0.4276`) are non-dominated among calibrated models. For ultra-low-latency online credit APIs (`~13–19 µs` per applicant) with frequent retraining, **`xgb_hist`** (or **`catboost`**) is optimal; for automated lending decisions where probability accuracy directly governs approval cutoffs, **`stack_lr`** (`42.1 ms/1k` = `42 µs`/loan, `Test PR 0.4279`, `Test Brier 0.1386`, `ECE 0.0045`) delivers the highest accuracy and calibration at negligible per-applicant latency. If regulatory adverse-action rules mandate strict feature monotonicity, deploy **`lgbm_monotone`** (`39.5 ms/1k`, `-0.0010` ROC cost) after constraining all income-coupled ratios.
